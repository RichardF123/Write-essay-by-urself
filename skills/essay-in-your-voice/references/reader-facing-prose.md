# Write for the finished text's reader

Use this check after sentence organization and before final delivery, especially when the user rejects defensive writing or explanations of old edits. The target is a direct, evidence-bounded argument in the author's voice.

## Keep revision reasoning in its proper output

The final essay presents the current argument, evidence and conclusions. Put explanations of changes, rejected candidates, old wording and reasons for removing an example in the revision log. Do not insert a new sentence into the essay merely to rebut an earlier assistant draft or editing suggestion.

Reflection assignments, revision memos and reviewer responses can explicitly require this history. Treat history as task content in those modes. A legitimate comparison of earlier scholarship is also part of an argument, not drafting leakage.

When retrieval is available, pass the writer the current brief, fixed content, applicable preferences and original style anchors. Keep rejected-candidate discussions in revision memory; the reviewer may consult them to identify newly introduced drafting explanations. Translate useful feedback into a current writing instruction rather than repeatedly injecting the whole editing debate.

## Review rhetorical function

Check the following as candidates, not proof of AI authorship or automatic deletion rules:

| Candidate | Ask | Action when justified |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `Unlike the previous draft...`, `This change avoids...` | Is revision history requested by this document's reader? | Move the explanation to the revision record; preserve the current argument. |
| `This does not mean X, nor Y...` | Which real objection, source or likely inference is being addressed? | Remove an unnecessary defense or state the same bounded claim directly. |
| `not merely X but Y` and repeated balanced contrasts | Do both halves add relevant information? | Preserve a genuine distinction; simplify staging or redundant emphasis. |
| `might possibly perhaps...` | Which words actually control certainty or scope? | Keep sufficient qualification and remove duplication. |
| A limitation repeated at every paragraph end | Has the same boundary already been stated clearly? | Keep it close to the claim where it matters, within scope. |
| Explanations of why a method was not used | Does rejecting that method affect the current interpretation? | Retain a substantive methodological point; move editing rationale out. |

For each proposed change, identify the exact span, its contribution, the objection or evidence it addresses, the effect on meaning, the original style anchor where applicable, and the permitted action. A sentence with no specific problem can remain, even when highlighted repeatedly.

## Preserve calibrated claims

Ask what changes if a phrase is removed: population, time, probability, causality, identity, attribution, or a substantive negative conclusion. Preserve those constraints. Prefer a compact limitation attached to its claim over several generic disclaimers.

Do not replace `may be` with `is`, association with causation, or an unresolved identity with a confirmed one. A positive rewrite must preserve the original propositions and claim strength. Do not invent a fact to make it sound more confident. Negation can carry essential analysis; the author's real counterargument is not filler.

This review respects frozen passages. Outside-scope issues are reported separately, not silently removed. In wording-only mode, deleting an existing substantive objection is an argument change; preserve it or report it. Removing a purely editorial aside is permitted only when it is inside the authorized span and the user's scope allows that correction.

## Examples

These are synthetic examples, not a universal personal style.

- **Editing history:** `This section compares two paintings. This avoids the previous draft's discussion of identity.` → `This section compares two paintings.` Put the editing reason in the log when relevant.
- **Unnecessary defense:** `I examine these two paintings. This does not mean other paintings have no value.` → `I examine these two paintings.` This assumes the second sentence is not part of a real debate about selection.
- **Repeated uncertainty:** `The figure might possibly be the owner.` → `The figure might be the owner.` Keep the unresolved identification.
- **Material limit:** `The survey shows an association; it does not establish causation.` → retain the inferential limit. A shorter rewrite needs the same meaning in context.
- **Required reflection:** `I removed an unsupported claim after revising my plan.` → retain when the task asks what changed and why.

## Research basis and interpretation

[Syntactic-template research](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.00211) motivates examining repeated structures, while [cross-genre stylistic analysis](https://aclanthology.org/2026.findings-emnlp.1375/) motivates context-dependent judgments. [UNC's guidance on qualifiers](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/qualifiers/) and [Manchester's cautious-language guidance](https://www.phrasebank.manchester.ac.uk/using-cautious-language/) support preserving useful epistemic limits and avoiding excess qualification. [Harvard's counterargument guidance, PDF page 6](https://writingproject.fas.harvard.edu/sites/g/files/omnuum4531/files/hwp/files/er_40_v2.pdf) distinguishes deliberation from what enters a finished essay.

These sources do not supply a detector rule or establish that all models overhedge. Revision-history leakage is a task-observed delivery problem addressed by this workflow. This reference defines editorial decisions rather than an automatic stylistic classifier.
